Samantha: Helen: Our team tested twenty-minute meetings instead of the usual forty-minute sessions. The aim was to see whether shorter meetings could preserve the information needed for decisions. We saved meeting time, but I would not yet say productivity increased. We measured minutes in meetings, not the quantity or quality of completed work. Those are different outcomes.

Daniel: Marcus: For me, the shorter format encouraged better preparation. I read the agenda and supporting documents beforehand, because I knew there would not be time to discover the whole topic during the meeting. My questions became more focused. However, I already know most of the projects well. Someone unfamiliar with the background might need more explanation than I do.

Karen: Nadia: That is my concern. New colleagues sometimes hesitate to interrupt, especially when they can see the meeting is nearly over. A short session may look efficient while leaving their questions unanswered. I would keep a short question period after the main discussion. People could raise points that are essential for understanding without extending every agenda item.

Samantha: Helen: I think the trial is worth continuing with adjustments. We should collect feedback from junior as well as experienced staff, and compare decisions and follow-up work across similar projects. We should also record whether another meeting becomes necessary because something was unclear. The early results are useful, but they are not enough to make twenty minutes a universal rule.
