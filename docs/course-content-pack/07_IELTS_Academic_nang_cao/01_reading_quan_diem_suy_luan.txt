WHEN A DASHBOARD LOOKS LIKE LEARNING
Schools increasingly use digital dashboards to monitor students' activity. A dashboard may display minutes spent on a platform, completed exercises and the number of correct answers. Supporters argue that such information helps teachers notice a struggling student before a final examination. This is a reasonable possibility, but a visible measure should not automatically be treated as a complete description of learning.
Consider time on a platform. A student may leave a page open while doing something unrelated, producing a long session without sustained attention. Another student may understand a task quickly and finish in a short session. The first record looks more impressive if duration is the only measure, although the second student may have learned more efficiently. Time can be useful context, but its meaning depends on what the student was doing.
Completion counts have similar limitations. Repeating familiar exercises may produce many completed tasks without requiring new understanding. Conversely, working carefully through one unfamiliar problem can involve substantial learning. A system that rewards completion alone may unintentionally encourage students to choose easy work. This does not make completion data worthless; it means that the difficulty and purpose of tasks must also be considered.
Some technology companies respond by combining many indicators into a single progress score. A combined score can simplify communication, but it can also conceal assumptions. If a system gives more weight to speed than to explanation, a thoughtful student may appear less successful. Teachers and students should therefore be able to ask what a score represents and which behaviours increase it. A number is easier to display than a complex explanation, yet ease of display is not evidence of validity.
The strongest use of dashboards is arguably as a starting point for inquiry. If a student's accuracy falls, a teacher can inspect the tasks and ask the student to explain an answer. An error might reflect a misunderstood concept, unfamiliar vocabulary or simply an accidental selection. These possibilities require different responses, even when the recorded outcome is identical. Data can help direct attention, while conversation and fresh assessment help interpret it.
None of this requires abandoning educational technology. The problem is not measurement itself but the temptation to make claims beyond what a measure supports. Schools can report that a learner completed a practice activity without claiming that the learner has mastered the underlying skill. They can also test understanding with unfamiliar questions after a delay. A dashboard designed around those distinctions is less dramatic than one promising to reveal learning instantly, but it is more useful for decisions about what to teach next.
