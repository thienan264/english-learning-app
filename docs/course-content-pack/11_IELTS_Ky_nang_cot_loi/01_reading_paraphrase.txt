LEARNING BEYOND THE LECTURE
At Eastport College, students on an introductory science course used to attend two long lectures each week. Most information came from the teacher, while students listened and took notes. A recent pilot replaced one lecture with a problem-solving workshop. Students still attended the other lecture, so the course did not remove teacher explanations entirely.
Before each workshop, students watched a short video and answered three preparation questions. In class, they worked in groups of four to solve a practical problem. The teacher moved between groups, asked questions and clarified misunderstandings. Students were expected to explain their reasoning, rather than simply copy a correct answer.
The first workshops were not immediately successful. Some students arrived without watching the video and depended on classmates to explain the background. In response, the teacher made the preparation questions part of the workshop's opening activity. This encouraged students to prepare and gave the teacher a quick indication of what needed explanation.
After eight weeks, participating students performed better on a task requiring them to apply ideas to an unfamiliar situation. Their scores on a factual recall test were similar to those of a previous class. The organisers therefore suggest that the workshops may help application more than memorisation. They also note that the classes were not randomly assigned, so the difference cannot be attributed to the teaching format alone.
Students reported that speaking in small groups felt less intimidating than asking questions in a large lecture hall. However, some wanted clearer roles within groups because one confident student sometimes dominated discussion. The college plans to keep the workshops while testing a system in which students rotate the roles of explaining, checking and recording. The pilot supports further investigation, rather than a claim that lectures should disappear.
